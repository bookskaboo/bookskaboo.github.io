Chapter 2

Not Another Isekai

◆

The first thing Claire saw was a bright blue sky.

The last thing she remembered began beyond a hotel window, where a pale blur hung above the city in the darkening twilight.

Claire first took it for a reflection of the amber lights behind her, but it remained fixed as she moved through the reception. It was too steady to be an aircraft and too bright to be a star.

She turned from the window and saw Daniel Mercer across the reception.

He stood in a conservative dark jacket over a slightly wrinkled dress shirt, as if he had come straight from work. Lanky and easy to overlook among the room’s polished guests, he wore his medium-brown hair slightly untidy despite the formal clothes. Subtle fatigue showed around his brown eyes and along the narrow lines of his face.

Even at rest, he held himself upright and self-contained—shoulders squared, hands quiet, jaw set as though the evening were another obligation to finish properly. When she reached him, his guarded expression eased.

The pale blur beyond the glass had grown larger. It flashed white.

The reception dissolved into light and startled cries. Claire turned toward Daniel because he was familiar, because he was there.

For one suspended heartbeat, his tired brown eyes found hers.

Then the light took him too.

Her view widened until overlapping leaves framed the sky. Sunlight filtered through the gaps. The canopy swayed—or perhaps her vision did.

Pain came next, tight around her ribs and hot across her back and shoulder. A shallow breath made it worse.

Sun-warmed timber pressed against her through her clothes. The boards flexed under her weight. Rope creaked nearby. Far below, insects rasped beneath the leaves, and the humid air carried the smell of resin and leaf mold.

A wooden dagger crossed one patch of sky in a slow turn and clattered near her feet.

She was lying on a training platform high in the canopy. Someone had knocked her flat.

Then she realized the body on the boards was not hers. Even lying still, its proportions felt wrong. Its shoulders rested too narrowly against the timber, and its legs felt lighter, their weight distributed along unfamiliar lines. Long hair slid across her mouth in a cool sheet far longer than it should have been.

A woman stepped into view.

She looked young, though her fine-boned face made her age difficult to judge. Silver hair had been tied away from slightly pointed ears, shining where the sunlight touched it, and the blunted practice blade in her hand rested against her thigh.

The face supplied a name—Mireth Thalewyn—and nothing else.

“Are you finished?” she asked.

Claire tried to inhale. Her ribs refused.

The woman’s mouth tightened. “Or did you decide even standing was more effort than you meant to give me today, Evynia?”

Evynia?

“Get up,” Mireth said.

Around them, the world came into focus by degrees.

The sparring floor was not on the ground. It was a broad platform fitted between the trunks of trees so large that three people holding hands could not have encircled one. Elevated walkways ran away through layers of sunlit green, some made from shaped planks and others from woven rope. Roofs with deep overhangs emerged among the branches. Below, glimpsed through curtains of vine and drifting haze, the forest floor broke around buttress roots and broad-leafed ferns.

Half a dozen onlookers waited around the platform. All had narrow faces, graceful builds, and ears that rose to subtle points. Most wore their silver hair braided, bound, or cut away from the face. Claire searched their faces for some reflection of her own disbelief. She found none. Only restrained disappointment.

Claire pressed both palms to the rough boards.

Breathe.

Find your center.

Move.

The familiar rhythm calmed her. Her breathing steadied, and the platform stopped pitching. She drew one knee under her, planted one foot, and stood.

Too quickly.

She straightened into a body several inches shorter than she expected. Her weight shifted forward sooner. She tried to correct by instinct, but the movement carried her farther off balance.

A hand caught her elbow before she could fall.

Claire pulled free.

Mireth’s eyes narrowed. “Did you hit your head?”

“I don’t think so.”

Her own voice stopped her. It was lighter than she remembered and carried a resonance she had never heard.

A few people at the platform’s edge exchanged glances.

Claire was accustomed to the admiration that followed a perfect performance. The doubt in these faces made her wish they would look away. With her body no longer answering as expected, she felt like she was eight years old again.

Claire picked up the dagger near her feet, then crossed to retrieve the second, grateful for a moment to try to gather herself.

As it settled into her grip, Claire tested its balance. The length and heft felt off compared to her foil. The blade in her left hand was useless to her.

Across the platform, Mireth reset her stance. “Again.”

Claire took her usual stance: body turned narrow, lead foot forward. Setting her feet was easy. The adjustments she had trained into instinct seemed to do nothing for her now.

She extended her right arm and drove off her back leg, sending her lead foot forward in a standard lunge.

Mireth’s eyes widened. Even surprised, she turned Claire’s dagger aside with a short movement of her wrist.

Claire’s lead foot landed where she meant it to. Her weight did not. Recovering her stance took conscious effort, and she spent too much attention on her balance and not enough on her opponent.

Mireth moved before she could recover.

Claire saw the shift in Mireth’s shoulder. The turn of her hip showed the attack’s direction.

Outside.

Claire stepped outside the line of the strike and turned as Mireth’s blade passed beside her.

Too slow.

Before Claire could finish the turn, Mireth caught her wrist, planted one foot across Claire’s path, and pulled. The motion turned Claire’s momentum against her and sent her pitching toward the boards. She tried to twist through the fall, but her knee struck first.

Pain flared through the joint, but this time she kept hold of both daggers. She felt Mireth’s blade against her neck.

“That,” she said, “was new.”

Claire kept her gaze on the boards.

“You surprised me,” Mireth continued.

“I was trying not to lose again.”

Mireth’s expression sharpened. “You don’t usually put in that kind of effort.”

Mireth offered a hand. Claire shifted both daggers into her left hand before accepting it and rising.

“You’re done for the morning.”

“I can continue.”

Mireth studied her, then looked past Claire. “She’s either hurt or finally ill.”

“Those are not the only explanations for unusual behavior,” a dry voice said.

Claire turned toward the speaker, careful of the ache along her ribs. An older woman stood beside the weapon rack.

“Evynia.”

Nymara Thalewyn.

Her gaze settled on the knee Claire had landed on, then on the daggers still in her hands.

“You have never argued for more training.”

Claire looked down at the wooden blades. “I wanted to understand what I did wrong.”

The elder studied her in silence, then stepped closer and lifted one finger before Claire’s eyes.

Claire had answered enough questions from physicians after bad landings to know what came next. She followed Nymara’s finger, told her where it hurt, and stood still while the elder checked the back of her head.

Claire returned the practice blades to the rack. Nymara nodded toward a walkway leading deeper into the settlement.

“Come. Walk with me before lunch.”

The concern beneath the order made Claire look away. “All right.”

They descended through Thalewyn together. The walkway curved around living trunks and crossed open air on rope and narrow planks. Below, steep roofs emptied through carved channels into cisterns. A worker had marked a warped board for replacement.

Patterns had been burned into the railings where passing hands would not wear them away. On a lower bridge, two workers replaced a split pin while foot traffic moved around them.

Children hurried past carrying covered bowls. A man working resin into a bridge joint called one back to retie a loose cord. The child obeyed and ran after the others.

At the wash space, clear water spilled from one carved basin to the next below an open wall. Claire knelt before a polished metal mirror hung from a roof beam.

A stranger looked back from the polished metal.

Her new face was narrow, with slim cheeks and slightly elongated ears rising through silver hair. Faint pink highlights caught the light when she moved. Her blue eyes held a restrained violet cast.

She was young and pretty, but nothing in the reflection resembled Claire Bennett.

Nymara stroked the pink-highlighted strands in Claire’s silver hair. “I thought you had made peace with what sets you apart. The pink in your hair and the violet cast in your eyes are rare, and a gold core rarer still. People saw promise and began naming your future before you could name it yourself.”

“So everyone decided what I should become,” Claire said.

“Many did. Your parents did not.” Nymara let the strands fall. “They stand among Thalewyn’s finest mercenaries and hoped you might follow their path, but wanted the choice to remain yours.”

Claire gave the unfamiliar reflection one last look, then headed down to lunch.

At a communal dining platform, people sat beneath a roof open on three sides to the warm forest air. Food passed hand to hand: steamed roots, smoked game wrapped in leaves, tart fruit, and a thick broth scented with unfamiliar herbs. Claire waited until the people nearest her began eating, then copied them.

No one asked whether she was hungry. Three different adults added food to her bowl.

The young man across from Claire set down his spoon. While the others ate, he had kept his attention on her.

“You’re quiet,” he said.

“She lost twice this morning, Tarin,” Mireth called from farther down the table. “We should appreciate the peace.”

He ignored her. “Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“You’re holding the spoon in your right hand.”

Claire followed his gaze.

Tarin gave the spoon a puzzled glance, then leaned toward Mireth, the promise of big news in his grin. “Did you hear? The southern hunt came back before dawn.”

Mireth looked up from her bowl. “All of them?”

“All six. One broken arm, but they brought back two cores.”

“They were sent after three nests,” Mireth said. “What happened to the last one?”

“Empty.” Tarin lowered his voice, though the words still came quickly. “The tracks came out of the west, from the Lakeheart side.”

Mireth’s spoon paused halfway to her mouth. “This far into the Reach?”

He nodded. “And the hunters were not the only ones who saw them. The Highbough Road escort crossed more tracks coming from the same direction. Their caravan reached the river market, but the pack animals fought their handlers whenever they tried to take the western trail.”

“What kind of tracks?”

“Predators and herd beasts. Kinds that stay close to Lakeheart, not this deep in the Reach. Too many of both, all using the same paths.”

Mireth set her spoon aside. “What did the elders do?”

“Called in both band leaders.”

“From their contracts?”

“Both of them. The western scouts too.”

Mireth leaned forward. “Nothing leaves its range in numbers like that without a reason.”

“There isn’t even a contract yet.”

“Then this isn’t another hunt. Something around Lakeheart is driving them into the Reach.”

Tarin nodded. “That’s what people are saying. Whatever it is, the elders wouldn’t pull two bands from paid work if they thought it was small.”

A grin spread across Mireth’s face. “We should volunteer.”

Tarin’s own smile faded. “We don’t even know what frightened them.”

“That’s what scouts are for.”

“They already called the western scouts.”

Mireth tilted her head. “And they may need more. You were excited a moment ago.”

“I am. I just don’t think we’re ready.”

Claire looked between them. “What happens if the elders call every band home?”

Mireth’s expression sobered. “Then Thalewyn answers.”

Bowls began moving toward the wash trough. Mireth stood while Tarin was still finishing his broth, and he hurried after her with the last piece of root in his hand.

Claire followed them across two walkways to a long room built against the curve of an enormous trunk. Woven mats covered most of the floor. Open latticework admitted warm air and green-filtered daylight, but no weapon racks broke the bare walls. The ordered space was the first part of the village that made immediate sense to her.

Younger trainees settled near the front. Some sat easily with their backs straight, while others shifted their knees or copied the posture of whoever sat nearest. Mireth and Tarin joined the oldest group at the rear without looking for direction. Claire took the open mat between them. Her knee ached when she lowered herself, and the first deep breath pulled at the bruised muscles along her ribs and upper back.

“Settle. We’re starting.”

The remaining conversation stopped.

The instructor stood at the front of the room. Ilyan Thalewyn. He was slender and appeared middle-aged. His left sleeve had been folded and secured against his side, and a patch covered his right eye. His remaining eye moved across the class without hurry.

He lowered himself onto a mat in one controlled motion. “Close your eyes. Find your core. Do not draw from it.”

Claire closed her eyes. The room disappeared. In its place came the pressure of the mat beneath her ankles, the ache in her knee, and the uneven pull around her ribs. Her breathing sounded too close. Her heartbeat seemed to move more of this smaller chest than it should.

Nymara had called it a gold core. Claire had understood the words. She had no idea what she was supposed to feel.

“Do not search with your muscles,” Ilyan said. “Let them rest.”

Claire released the tension from her shoulders. Her posture sagged, and she corrected it before forcing herself to relax again. Breath passed in and out. The discomfort remained. Beneath it, something warmer held steady.

She turned her attention toward it. The sensation sharpened until her mind gave it shape: a sphere of golden light, suspended somewhere inside her. She did not see it with her eyes, yet the image was clear enough to make the darkness behind her eyelids seem brighter.

The sensation was new. The name came readily.

Her core.

“Once you have it, open the flow,” Ilyan said. “A small amount. Carry it through the body.”

Claire reached for the light. It answered at once.

The golden light spread outward from her core, filling her torso while the sphere remained bright at its center. Warmth followed the color through her chest and abdomen, fading at her shoulders and hips.

Her arms and legs remained untouched.

Claire tried to carry the flow into her limbs. Nothing moved.

She drew more.

The sphere brightened. Gold surged into her right shoulder and down her arm. Strength came with it, but by the time the color reached her fingers, they had begun to tremble.

“Evynia. Close it.”

Claire pulled away from the light. The warmth vanished so quickly that her arm felt abruptly heavier without it. Something remained behind: a hollow pressure where the brightness had been, like holding her breath after emptying her lungs.

She opened her eyes. Ilyan watched her from the front of the room.

“I said a small amount.”

“I thought more would spread farther.”

“More drains faster. It does not make you precise.” His gaze moved across the younger trainees before returning to her. “That hollow feeling is depletion. Ignore it long enough and you will lose consciousness. Your core will recover. Not quickly enough to reward waste.”

Claire studied her right hand. The comparison formed easily: a battery that refilled on its own, connected to a switch she could only turn fully on or off. She had found the switch. She had no control over the current.

Ilyan rose. “Watch.”

Ilyan’s posture tightened all at once. Nothing shone around him, yet the change was too complete to be a breath or shift of balance.

He crossed the width of two mats in a single step and stopped without a sound. The reinforcement disappeared.

A heavy wooden block stood near the wall. Ilyan slid his remaining hand beneath it, engaged his mana, and lifted it to his hip. His frame showed none of the strain the weight should have required. He lowered the block, and the tension left him as abruptly as it had come.

“Mireth.”

She rose and crossed to him.

“Push.”

Mireth set both palms against his right shoulder. His posture tightened in the same abrupt change. She drove forward, legs braced and shoulders low. Ilyan did not shift.

He released the reinforcement and stepped back with her pressure. “Speed. Power. Toughness. The mana is the same. What it fills determines what it strengthens.”

Claire watched him return to his mat. The change had been clean. On. Off. No tremor, no visible strain, no pause afterward.

“Oldest group,” Ilyan said. “Full body.”

Mireth and Tarin closed their eyes and settled their breathing. A moment later, tension set through both of them at once—the same abrupt transition Ilyan had shown.

“Release.”

The tension left them together.

“Again.”

They obeyed with the ease of a familiar drill. Claire closed her eyes before either could look at her.

Claire found the golden sphere again. Her frustration rose as soon as she touched it. She had drawn too much.

Gymnastics had taught her to repeat a motion until it became familiar.

Claire chose the path she had already found. She opened the flow and held the draw steady when the heat reached her right shoulder. It still came too quickly, but she kept it from growing.

She carried it to the elbow, then the wrist. Her fingers warmed, and strength returned to the arm, steadier than before.

Claire closed the flow. The heat faded. The hollow feeling deepened by a small, measurable amount.

Again.

She repeated the path until she could interrupt the surge. Then she did the same with her left arm.

She let the heat fade and began with her legs. The new proportions made the internal map feel wrong. Her hips sat closer to the floor than she expected. Her legs ended sooner. Each time habit carried her attention toward the shape of her old body, the mana slipped or gathered in the wrong place.

Claire corrected the map and tried again.

Across the room, Ilyan moved among the younger trainees. He told one to release a clenched jaw and another to stop pulling after the mana reached his feet. His corrections were brief. He never repeated one until the student had made another attempt.

By the time he returned to the oldest group, sweat had gathered beneath Claire’s hair.

“Full body,” he said.

Mireth and Tarin engaged their mana.

Claire drew the smallest amount she could manage. Heat spread through her torso and reached her shoulders. She divided her attention, guiding it down both arms while carrying it through her hips. The flow wavered.

She held it.

The heat moved down her legs. Her injured knee remained sore, but the muscles around it tightened and steadied the joint. Mana reached her ankles, then her feet.

Her fingers were warm. So were her toes.

For one breath, the flow held throughout her body. Every muscle felt more capable. Not trained. Not coordinated. Stronger.

Claire closed the flow. The heat vanished.

She opened her eyes with a smile already forming.

Ilyan stood over her.

“Too much,” he said.

The smile faded. “It was even.”

“It was,” he said. “It was also far more than you needed.”

Claire felt the hollow space inside her and knew he was right.

“You have never worked this hard in my class,” he said.

She offered no response.

Ilyan turned away. “Better late than never.”

The dismissal came soon afterward.

Claire followed the upper walkways behind the other trainees, watching which paths led toward the sleeping quarters.

She hesitated at one fork. A passing woman glanced at her, then pointed up a short stair.

At the top, one doorway felt familiar when she reached it. The room was small and orderly, with a narrow bed beneath the sloping roof, folded clothes on an open shelf, and two empty dagger sheaths hanging from a peg. Nothing in it felt like hers.

Claire sat on the edge of the bed and flexed her right hand. The added strength was gone, but she could still trace the path the warmth had taken from the golden core to her fingertips.

She wanted to be strong again, with a body and core that answered exactly as she intended.

Balance first. Then strength and endurance. Mana in smaller draws until she could fill the body without wasting most of what she drew.

The plan carried the same excitement she remembered from childhood, when holding a position for one second longer had been enough to make her try again.

Tomorrow, Claire would start from the beginning.
