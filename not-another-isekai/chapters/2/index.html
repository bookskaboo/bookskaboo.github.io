Chapter 2

Not Another Isekai

◆

The white light faded into a bright blue sky.

Why is it daytime?

Only a moment ago, Claire had been standing at a hotel reception as evening settled over the city. Something pale had hung beyond the window.

She had first taken it for a reflection of the amber lights behind her, but it remained fixed as she moved among the guests. It was too steady to be an aircraft and too bright to be a star.

She turned from the window and saw Daniel Mercer across the room.

He stood in a conservative dark jacket over a slightly wrinkled dress shirt, as if he had come straight from work. Lanky and easy to overlook, he wore his brown hair slightly untidy despite the formal clothes. Subtle fatigue marked his lean face.

Even at rest, he held himself upright and self-contained—shoulders squared, hands quiet, jaw set as though the event were another obligation to finish properly. When she reached him, his guarded expression eased.

The pale blur beyond the glass had grown larger. It flashed white.

The reception dissolved into light and startled cries. Claire turned toward Daniel because he was familiar, because he was there.

For one suspended heartbeat, his brown eyes found hers.

Then the light took him too.

The sky returned in fragments between overlapping leaves. Sunlight filtered through the gaps.

Pain tightened around her ribs and burned across her back and shoulder. Trying to draw breath made it worse.

Sun-warmed timber pressed against her through her clothes. The boards flexed under her weight. Rope creaked nearby. Far below, insects rasped beneath the leaves, and the humid air carried the smell of resin and leaf mold.

A wooden dagger crossed one patch of sky in a slow turn and clattered near her feet.

She lay on a training platform high among the trees. Someone had knocked her flat.

Everything seemed off. Her shoulders rested too narrowly against the timber, and her legs felt too light. Long hair slid across her mouth in a cool sheet far longer than it should have been.

A woman stepped into view.

She looked young, though her fine-boned face made her age difficult to judge. Silver hair had been tied away from slightly pointed ears, shining where the sunlight touched it, and the blunted practice blade in her hand rested against her thigh.

The face supplied a name—Mireth Thalewyn—and nothing else.

“Are you finished?” she asked.

Mireth’s mouth tightened. “Or did you decide even standing was more effort than you meant to give me today, Evynia?”

Evynia?

“Get up,” Mireth said.

Claire looked past Mireth.

The platform stretched between trunks so wide that three people holding hands could not have encircled one. Walkways ran through layers of sunlit green, and roofs with deep overhangs emerged among the branches. The sounds of people carried through the leaves.

Half a dozen onlookers waited around the platform. They shared narrow faces, graceful builds, and subtly pointed ears. Claire searched their faces for some reflection of her own disbelief. She found only restrained disappointment.

Claire pressed both palms to the rough boards.

Breathe.

Find your center.

Move.

The familiar rhythm calmed her. Her breathing steadied, and the platform stopped pitching. She drew one knee under her, planted one foot, and stood.

Wrong.

She came upright several inches shorter than expected and pitched forward. She tried to correct by instinct, but the movement carried her farther off balance.

A hand caught her elbow before she could fall.

Claire tried to pull free.

Mireth let go, her eyes narrowing. “Did you hit your head?”

“I don’t think so.”

Was that me?

The voice was lighter than she remembered and carried a resonance she had never heard.

The onlookers’ doubt was nothing like the admiration that normally followed Claire’s performances. With her body no longer obeying her, she felt self-conscious, like she was eight years old again.

Claire picked up the dagger near her feet, then spotted the other across the platform. She went to retrieve it, grateful for a moment to gather herself.

Claire adjusted her grip on the dagger in her right hand. Its length and heft felt wrong compared to the foil she had mastered in college. The blade in her left hand was useless to her.

Across the platform, Mireth reset her stance. “Again.”

Claire took her usual stance: body turned narrow, lead foot forward. Setting her feet was easy. The adjustments she had trained into instinct seemed to do nothing for her now.

She extended her right arm and drove off her back leg, sending her lead foot forward in a standard lunge.

Mireth’s eyes widened, but she moved deftly enough to turn Claire’s dagger aside.

Claire’s lead foot landed where she intended. Her weight did not. Recovering her stance took conscious effort, and she spent too much attention on her balance and not enough on her opponent.

Mireth moved before she could recover.

Claire saw the shift in Mireth’s shoulder. The turn of her hip showed the attack’s direction.

Outside.

Claire stepped outside the line of the strike and turned as Mireth’s blade passed beside her.

Too slow.

Before Claire could finish the turn, Mireth caught her wrist, planted one foot across Claire’s path, and pulled. The motion turned Claire’s momentum against her and sent her pitching toward the boards. She tried to twist through the fall, but her knee struck first.

Pain flared through the joint, but this time she kept hold of both daggers. She felt Mireth’s blade against her neck.

“That,” Mireth said, “was new.”

Claire kept her gaze on the boards.

“I was trying not to lose again.”

Mireth’s expression sharpened. “You don’t usually put in that kind of effort.”

Mireth pulled her up by the forearm. Claire winced.

“You’re done for the morning.”

“I can continue.”

“Evynia,” a voice called.

Claire turned toward the speaker, careful of the ache along her ribs. An older woman stood beside the weapon rack.

Nymara Thalewyn.

Her gaze settled on the knee Claire had landed on, then on the daggers still in her hands.

“You have never argued for more training.”

Claire looked down at the wooden blades. “I wanted to understand what I did wrong.”

The elder studied her in silence, then stepped closer and lifted one finger before Claire’s eyes.

Claire had answered enough questions from physicians after bad landings to know what came next. She followed Nymara’s finger, told her where it hurt, and stood still while the elder checked the back of her head.

Claire returned the practice blades to the rack. Nymara nodded toward a walkway leading deeper into the settlement.

“Come. Walk with me before lunch.”

“All right.”

They descended through Thalewyn together. The walkway curved around living trunks and crossed open air on a bridge made of rope and planks. The first shift beneath Claire’s feet sent her hand to the railing. Leaves brushed her shoulder where the walkway narrowed. Below, steep roofs emptied through carved channels into cisterns. A worker had marked a warped board for replacement.

Patterns had been burned into the railings where passing hands would not wear them away. On a lower bridge, two workers replaced a split pin while foot traffic moved around them.

At the wash space, clear water spilled from one carved basin to the next below an open wall. Claire knelt before a polished metal mirror hung from a roof beam.

Claire studied her reflection.

Her face was narrow, with slim cheeks and slightly elongated ears rising through silver hair. Faint pink highlights caught the light when she moved. Her blue eyes held a restrained violet cast.

She was young and pretty. Claire turned her head, watching the unfamiliar face follow the movement, then looked down at her shoulders and arms.

Pointed ears, silver hair, a village in the trees. None of it troubled her as much as the memory of stumbling across the training platform. She frowned at her reflection.

Nymara stroked the pink-highlighted strands in Claire’s silver hair. “I thought you had made peace with what sets you apart. The pink in your hair and the violet cast in your eyes are rare, and a gold core rarer still. People saw promise and began naming your future before you could name it yourself.”

“What did they expect me to do?” Claire asked.

“Follow your parents into mercenary work. They stand among Thalewyn’s finest.” Nymara let the strands fall. “They hoped you might choose their path, but never meant to choose it for you.”

At a communal dining platform, people sat beneath a roof open on three sides to the warm forest air. Food passed hand to hand: steamed roots, smoked game wrapped in leaves, tart fruit, and a thick broth scented with unfamiliar herbs. Claire waited until the people nearest her began eating, then copied them.

No one asked whether she was hungry. Three different adults added food to her bowl.

The young man across from Claire set down his spoon. While the others ate, he had kept his attention on her.

“You’re quiet,” he said.

“She lost twice this morning, Tarin,” Mireth called from farther down the table. “We should appreciate the peace.”

He ignored her. “Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“You’re holding the spoon in your right hand.”

Tarin’s puzzled gaze left the spoon, his expression brightening as he leaned toward Mireth. “Did you hear? The southern hunt came back before dawn.”

Mireth looked up from her bowl. “All of them?”

“All six. One broken arm, but they brought back two cores.”

“They were sent after three nests,” Mireth said. “What happened to the last one?”

“Empty.” Tarin lowered his voice, though the words still came quickly. “The tracks came out of the west, from the Lakeheart side.”

Mireth’s spoon paused halfway to her mouth. “This far into the Reach?”

He nodded. “And the hunters were not the only ones who saw them. The Highbough Road escort crossed more tracks coming from the same direction. Their caravan reached the river market, but the pack animals fought their handlers whenever they tried to take the western trail.”

“What kind of tracks?”

“Predators and herd beasts. Kinds that stay close to Lakeheart. Too many of both, all using the same paths.”

Mireth set her spoon aside. “What did the elders do?”

“Two bands were called home.”

“From their contracts?”

“Both of them. The western scouts too.”

Mireth leaned forward. “Something strange must be happening at Lakeheart.”

“There isn’t even a contract yet.”

“It must be serious if they’re pulling two bands from paid work.”

A grin spread across Mireth’s face. “We should volunteer.”

Tarin’s own smile faded. “We don’t even know what’s happening.”

“That’s what scouts are for.”

“They already called the western scouts.”

Mireth tilted her head. “And they may need more. You were excited a moment ago.”

“I am. I just don’t think we’re ready.”

Bowls began moving toward the wash trough. Mireth stood while Tarin was still finishing his broth, and he hurried after her with the last piece of root in his hand.

Claire followed them across two walkways to a long room built against the curve of an enormous trunk. Woven mats covered most of the floor. Open latticework admitted warm air and green-filtered daylight, but no weapon racks broke the bare walls. The ordered space was the first part of the village that made immediate sense to her.

Younger trainees settled near the front. Some sat easily with their backs straight, while others shifted their knees or copied the posture of whoever sat nearest. Mireth and Tarin joined the oldest group at the rear without looking for direction. Claire took the open mat between them.

“Silence. We’re starting.”

The instructor stood at the front of the room. Ilyan Thalewyn. He was slender and appeared middle-aged. His left sleeve had been folded and secured against his side, and a patch covered his right eye. His remaining eye moved across the class without hurry.

“Close your eyes. Find your core. Do not draw from it.”

Claire closed her eyes. A gold core. She had understood Nymara’s words, but had no idea what she was looking for.

“Relax your body,” Ilyan said. “Search deep within.”

She let her shoulders relax and concentrated on her breathing. Her knee ached. Each time it distracted her, she brought her attention back inward, until she found a steady warmth at her center.

She focused on the warmth. The sensation sharpened until her mind gave it shape: a sphere of golden light.

“Once you have it, expand it,” Ilyan said. “Slowly. Let it fill your body.”

Claire focused on the sphere and tried to make it grow.

Its golden boundary widened through her torso. Warmth filled her chest and abdomen evenly, but the expansion stopped short of her arms and legs.

Claire tried to expand the sphere farther, but it refused to budge.

She pushed harder.

The gold brightened without spreading. Heat built through her torso, and her back straightened with unexpected strength. Her hands began to tremble against her knees.

“Evynia. Release it.”

Claire let the sphere contract. The warmth vanished, and holding herself upright took more effort again. A hollow pressure remained at her center, like holding her breath after emptying her lungs.

She opened her eyes. Ilyan watched her.

“What happened?”

“It got hotter, but it wouldn’t grow.”

“Then try gently. Forcing more mana into the same space will only empty your core.” His gaze moved across the class. “That hollow feeling is depletion. Continue until your core is empty and you will lose consciousness. It recovers with rest. Give it time.”

So it’s a battery that recharges itself, and reinforcement is the light switch. I just have to figure out how to turn it all the way on.

“Watch,” Ilyan said.

Ilyan’s posture tightened all at once. Nothing shone around him, yet the change was too complete to be a breath or shift of balance.

He crossed the width of two mats in a single step and stopped without a sound. The reinforcement disappeared.

A heavy wooden block stood near the wall. Ilyan slid his remaining hand beneath it, engaged his mana, and lifted it to his hip. His frame showed none of the strain the weight should have required. He lowered the block, and the tension left him as abruptly as it had come.

“Mireth.”

She rose and crossed to him.

“Strike my shoulder.”

Mireth drew back her fist. Ilyan’s posture tightened just before she punched.

She hissed and shook out her hand, unsurprised, as though she had been told to punch a tree trunk.

He released the reinforcement. “Speed. Power. Toughness. The mana is the same. What it fills determines what it strengthens.”

Mireth returned to her place.

“Oldest group,” Ilyan said. “Full body.”

Mireth and Tarin closed their eyes and settled their breathing. A moment later, both of them tightened into the reinforced posture Ilyan had shown.

“Release.”

The tension left them together.

“Again.”

They obeyed with the ease of a familiar drill. Claire closed her eyes before either could look at her.

I can do this.

Claire found the golden sphere again.

Gymnastics had taught her to repeat a motion until it became familiar.

Claire let the sphere widen through her torso again. When the gold began to brighten, she eased her effort. Its boundary stopped, but the warmth stayed even.

She held it for a breath, then tried to expand it a little farther.

She released it. The heat faded, leaving only a slight increase in the hollow feeling.

Again.

She expanded the sphere and released it, over and over. Sometimes it brightened before it grew. Sometimes it contracted as soon as she eased her effort.

The warmth gradually reached farther into her shoulders and hips. Each attempt gave her a clearer sense of the difference between making the sphere larger and making it stronger.

She tried again, keeping the gold as steady as she could.

Across the room, Ilyan moved among the younger trainees. He told one to release a clenched jaw and another to stop forcing the expansion. His corrections were brief. He never repeated one until the student had made another attempt.

By the time he returned to the oldest group, sweat had gathered beneath Claire’s hair.

“Full body,” he said.

Mireth and Tarin engaged their mana.

Claire found the sphere and let it expand. Gold filled her torso, then widened through her arms and legs. She eased her effort as the warmth grew stronger, careful not to let the boundary contract.

The warmth reached her hands and feet.

For one breath, warmth filled her whole body evenly. Every muscle felt stronger, though she still had to learn how to use them.

She released the sphere. The heat vanished, leaving her panting.

She opened her eyes with a smile already forming.

Ilyan stood over her.

“Too much,” he said.

“I reached my hands and feet,” she said.

“You did. Now learn to do it with less mana.”

Claire felt the hollow space inside her and knew he was right.

Claire followed the upper walkways behind the other trainees, watching which paths led toward the sleeping quarters.

She hesitated at one fork. A passing woman glanced at her, then pointed up a short stair.

At the top, one doorway felt familiar when she reached it. The room was small and orderly, with a narrow bed beneath the sloping roof, folded clothes on an open shelf, and two empty dagger sheaths hanging from a peg. Nothing in it felt like hers.

Claire sat on the edge of the bed and flexed her right hand. The added strength was gone, but she remembered the warmth filling her from her center to her fingertips.

Balance first. Then strength and endurance. Practice expanding the sphere—her mana core—until she could reinforce her whole body without wasting so much mana.

The plan carried the same excitement she remembered from childhood, when holding a position for one second longer had been enough to make her try again.

Tomorrow, Claire would start from the beginning.

End of Chapter 2
