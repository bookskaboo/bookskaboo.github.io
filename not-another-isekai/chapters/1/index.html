Chapter 1

Not Another Isekai

◆

Pain came first.

Something hard cracked across the back of Daniel’s head. His eyes snapped open to a scarred wooden workbench inches from his face. Brass filings dusted the dark grain. A loose fitting rolled through them and stopped beside a stack of papers.

“Up.”

The voice was low, rough, and much too close.

Daniel stayed braced over the bench while the room assembled itself around him. A faint trace of lamp oil hung in the air. Nearby, a hand file scraped coarsely against metal. Afternoon light slanted through high windows and flashed along hanging tools. Below the floor, canal machinery knocked in a steady rhythm. A sharp, throbbing pulse spread from the base of his skull.

I have no idea where I am.

Panic followed a moment later—a feeling he knew well enough to set aside while he assessed the situation.

He was seated. He was breathing. He could move both hands. No blood that he could feel. His hearing worked, though his right ear rang.

These are not my hands.

They were leaner than he remembered, their knuckles marked by small burns and old cuts. A pale lock of hair hung across his vision. When he raised his head, it swung with him.

Is that blonde hair? My hair is brown.

A thick, callused hand came down on the workbench. The man above him was compact and broad-shouldered, with iron-gray hair and a close-trimmed beard. Heavy brows shadowed his eyes. He held a large wooden ruler in his other hand.

So that was what I was hit with.

The detail was absurd enough to steady him.

“Lucan,” the man said. “If your work interests you so little that you can sleep on it, perhaps the canal mud will prove more stimulating.”

The words sounded foreign, yet he understood them with the effortless fluency of a native speaker. The name struck something inside Daniel harder than the ruler had.

He thinks I’m Lucan.

Daniel sat upright and studied the man.

The face was unfamiliar, yet somehow Daniel knew the name: Severan Corvane.

Nothing else followed. His severe expression reminded Daniel of strict professors and project leads who expected discipline before explanations.

Severan’s eyes narrowed. “Have I broken something?”

“No,” Daniel said.

The voice was not his. It was younger and lighter.

None of this is mine.

At another bench, a short, stocky woman worked a narrow file through the grooves of a metal plate. Her dark auburn hair was pulled into a tight braid. A frown sat so naturally on her face that Daniel could not tell whether she was actually annoyed. Her gray-green eyes lifted briefly toward Daniel, but the file never stopped.

Beside a wall of shallow drawers, a tall, lanky man with dark curls turned from his work. He said nothing, but concern was easy to read on his otherwise boyish, carefree face.

Only their names registered: Sabine Arvello and Nerio Vale. Daniel set them aside; Severan still loomed over him.

Neither seemed surprised by Severan’s reprimand.

Daniel looked down.

A thick sheet of paper covered the front of the bench, filled with a machine drawing Daniel could only partly understand. A black ink pot sat near one corner, with brass filings scattered farther back. Behind the drawing, a slotted frame lay partly assembled among the loose components. One opening matched the fitting Daniel had knocked aside.

Beyond the unfinished frame, a cloudy red sphere rested within a broad cradle of brass and blackened steel. Heavy clamps held it in place. Shimmering white light traced channels over its surface and into the surrounding assembly. Beside the cradle, the shaft and gearing remained still.

I am looking at a mana core.

I have no idea how it works, and I know better than to touch it carelessly.

The drawing connected the core’s cradle to an unfamiliar assembly, then to a pump. Daniel could follow the shaft and gearing; he had made his living as a mechanical engineer. How mana became motion remained beyond him.

A muted wonder stirred beneath his confusion. On Earth, such a machine had existed only in fantasy.

Severan tapped the ruler against the unfinished drawing. “Do you remember where you left off?”

Daniel searched for something Lucan might say instead, but found nothing.

“No.”

Severan’s gaze moved to the smear of ink where Daniel’s cheek had rested. “You fell asleep at an active bench. Not knowing where you stopped makes it worse.”

I can explain the pain. Not the rest.

“Stand,” Severan said.

Daniel pushed himself to his feet. His center of gravity was lower than expected, his shoulders broader, his legs more responsive. He braced himself against the bench, but Severan saw the sway.

Severan set the ruler on the bench, gripped Daniel’s chin, and turned his face into the nearest sunbeam. Dust drifted between them while the master checked his eyes and scalp without gentleness.

“Eyes clear. No bleeding. Can you count?”

“Yes.”

“An improvement already.”

At the drawers, Nerio coughed into his fist, stifling a laugh.

Severan released him. “Then count the failures in your design before we get to discover them underwater.”

Severan slid the diagram toward Daniel.

Daniel studied the drawing again. Unmarked revisions crossed one another, and precise dimensions gave way abruptly to estimates. Much of it meant nothing to him, but he could follow some parts and see where they might interfere with each other.

“Some of these parts look too close together,” Daniel said. “Do those channels respond to heat?”

Silence.

Severan’s mouth tightened. “They do. How many faults have you found?”

I won’t guess.

“I don’t know yet.”

Across the bay, Sabine’s eyes narrowed on Daniel without breaking the rhythm of her work.

Severan studied Daniel. “You usually answer before I finish asking.”

“I can guess now,” Daniel said, “or check the channel layout and whatever test records you keep for the core.”

“The core rating,” Severan corrected. “Sit. Stay awake. Start with the channel layout. Do not touch the core or its channels.”

Daniel sat and fixed his attention on the page despite the throbbing in his head.

Over the next several hours, he fell into the methodical habits of his old work, taking care to note what made no sense in the drawing. It showed carved patterns on the core and the parts fitted around it. He did not yet understand the rules, but he could see the potential for danger.

Once the work had settled into manageable questions, Daniel allowed himself to study the workshop around him. Tools lined the walls beneath smoke-darkened beams, and mechanisms in various stages of repair filled the open bays. Tall wooden shutters stood open, revealing the canal beyond. Deeper inside, the benches stood farther apart, and heavy doors divided the work areas. Bare floor isolated the shielded test stands from the rest of the workshop.

He recognized a stripped floodgate mechanism and a sediment-scored pump almost at once. Warming plates, cooling chests, lamps, and weapon fittings crowded the smaller benches. But a nest of brass rings around an empty core socket told him nothing, nor did a sealed ceramic cylinder with no visible controls. Artificing plainly extended far beyond the civic project before him.

A hammer rang above the steady rasp of files. From below, water knocked against the foundations. Sabine continued filing a groove in the metal plate, pausing after each pass to check its depth. Severan moved through the bays, stopping to speak quietly with the other apprentices. When he passed Daniel, he said nothing.

Daniel traced the pressure line through the drawing. A thinner branch left it just before the valve and looped back toward the intake—the bypass. He checked every connection back to the intake before marking it.

As sunset turned the high windows amber, Severan stopped Daniel at the door and pressed the stained drawing into his hands.

“Redo it.”

Severan handed him two more sheets. “Identify every failure path you can justify. Order the tests so they risk neither the core, the mechanism, nor anyone nearby. Have it on my bench before work begins tomorrow.”

That, at least, is a language I understand.

Daniel took the sheets without complaint. “It will be ready.”

Severan lingered for a moment, as though expecting more, then moved on.

Outside, Nerio waited for Daniel beneath the workshop’s stone arcade. The last light picked out his warm brown eyes. He held a leather tool roll in weathered, callused hands.

Nerio glanced at the papers under Daniel’s arm. “No argument?”

“It is the work I was given.”

Nerio looked at him more closely. “Can you get home safely?”

Not in any way I can explain.

“My head hurts.”

That much was true.

Nerio nodded once. “Go home, then. I’ll finish closing the test bay. Keep to the streets, and sit down if the dizziness worsens—preferably before you reach a canal.”

Finding his way should have been impossible. Yet at each intersection, one street looked familiar enough for him to proceed.

A narrow street led from Corvane’s workshop between vaulted warehouses. Their open bays smelled of damp rope, sawn timber, grain dust, and river mud. When the street opened beside the canal, Daniel slowed to take in the view.

Varenza—the city Lucan had called home. From a stone bridge, Daniel could see red-tiled roofs stepping down through arcades and courtyards toward a web of locks. Beyond the city, the Velis descended from the mountains before spreading through Varenza like blue-green seams in pale stone. Barges moved through the wider channels while smaller boats passed between homes and workshops.

They had made the waterworks part of the city’s beauty rather than hiding them.

Below the bridge, clear green water pressed against the lock gates and leaked through their seams. Workers cleared silt from an overflow basin while a core-lit lifting frame raised a dripping basket onto the quay. Ropes creaked as two workers steadied it by hand.

Mana is integrated into the machinery. I wonder how many applications are possible.

Past the bridge, arcades shaded shops and mosaic thresholds. Cracks marked some of the lowest walls, and broad stone steps kept doorways above the canals. Merchants called across the water from stalls and balconies. Twice people greeted Lucan by name: a canal worker saluted, and a fruit seller asked about his family without waiting for more than Daniel’s cautious nod.

Farther on, a public fountain spilled into a basin tiled blue and green. Children trailed their fingers through the overflow while merchants packed the stalls, their voices mingling with the steady rush of water.

Sunlight scattered over the wet tiles like chandelier light through cut glass. The market’s murmur blurred into music and the chime of glasses on trays.

He remembered a grand hotel ballroom crowded with well-dressed guests beneath crystal chandeliers.

The conditioned air was cool and dry. He had attended only because declining would have required more explanation than appearing.

At thirty-two, he had come straight from work to the reception, wearing the dark jacket he kept in his office for formal events. Amber lights glowed above polished floors. Music mingled with the low roar of conversation. Servers moved between groups with bright glasses on black trays, and the city beyond the windows glittered without revealing a single star.

He had checked his phone twice in five minutes when Claire Bennett joined him. She had the practiced poise of someone accustomed to the spotlight.

Her black hair and dark brows stood in sharp contrast to her fair skin. Her expression was composed, more reserved than stern. The clean lines of her dress revealed her toned shoulders and arms.

Her athletic career had shaped that strength and the precise control with which she carried herself.

Quiet amusement softened her eyes and smile, and the polished public version of her became the Claire he had come to know over the years. Their conversations had a habit of resuming where the last one had ended, as though the time between them had been only a small interruption.

“There’s my lifesaver.”

“Glad to see you’re doing well.”

The nickname came from their previous meeting, more than a year earlier. After a charity gala, Daniel had found Claire standing beside her stalled car in the hotel garage. He had traced the fault under the hood and gotten the engine running again.

Back in the ballroom, they stood together near the room’s edge, enjoying the warmth of their brief exchange.

Then the amber lights gave way to something brighter.

White flooded the windows, the polished floor, the moving crowd. Voices broke into startled cries. Daniel’s eyes moved toward Claire through the glare and met hers.

The crowd blurred into pale silhouettes and vanished. Claire remained for one suspended heartbeat, her face the only shape left in the world, before the white swallowed her too.

Then pain. A wooden ruler. A stranger’s blonde hair falling across his eyes.

End of Chapter 1
