Chapter 1

Not Another Isekai

◆

Pain came first.

Something hard cracked against Daniel’s skull just behind his right ear. His eyes snapped open to a wooden workbench filling his vision, its dark grain scarred by old tool marks and dusted with brass filings. A loose brass fitting rolled past the scattered filings and stopped beside a stack of papers.

“Up.”

The voice was low, rough, and much too close.

Daniel stayed braced over the bench while the room assembled itself around him. A faint trace of lamp oil hung in the air. Nearby, a hand file scraped coarsely against metal. Afternoon light slanted through high windows and flashed along hanging tools. Below the floor, canal machinery knocked in a steady rhythm. A sharp, throbbing pulse spread from the base of his skull.

I have no idea where I am.

He reached that conclusion quickly. Panic followed a moment later—a feeling he knew well enough to set aside while he assessed the situation.

He was seated. He was breathing. He could move both hands. No blood that he could feel. His hearing worked, though his right ear rang.

These are not my hands.

They were leaner than he remembered, their knuckles marked by small burns and old cuts. A pale lock of hair hung across his vision. When he raised his head, it swung with him.

Is that blonde hair? My hair is brown.

A broad hand came down on the workbench. It belonged to a compact, solidly built man with iron-gray hair and a full beard trimmed close, heavy brows, and thick, callused fingers that made every tool nearby look fragile. He held what looked like a large wooden ruler in the other hand.

So that was what I was hit with.

The detail was absurd enough to steady him.

“Lucan,” the man said. “If your work interests you so little that you can sleep on it, perhaps the canal mud will prove more stimulating.”

The words sounded foreign, yet he understood them with the effortless fluency of a native speaker. The name struck something inside Daniel harder than the ruler had.

He thinks my name is Lucan.

The face belonged to a stranger, yet Daniel recognized it. Severan Corvane.

The name offered no context, but Severan’s expression reminded Daniel of strict professors and project leads who expected discipline before explanations.

Daniel sat upright and stared at Severan.

Severan’s eyes narrowed. “Have I broken something?”

“No,” Daniel said.

The voice was not his. It was younger and lighter.

He amended his list.

None of this is mine.

Across the workshop, a tall woman with dark auburn hair looked up from a regulating plate clamped to her bench. The narrow file in her hand had stopped mid-stroke, but her back remained straight and her expression closed. Chisels, gauges, and other tools lay in precise rows across her bench.

Sabine Arvello.

A man beside a wall of shallow drawers had turned toward Daniel with a coil of copper-colored strip in his hands. He was sturdily built, with dark curls falling around his face. His mouth had opened as if to speak, but he held back. His weight had shifted forward, ready to step closer.

Nerio Vale.

Daniel knew the names and nothing more. He compared Sabine’s controlled stillness with Nerio’s forward lean, searching for some shared clue. Neither seemed surprised by Severan’s reprimand. Their attention remained fixed on Daniel, as though his response—not the blow—was what had become unusual.

Nerio tilted his head. “You did hit him rather hard.” The humor in his voice did not hide his concern.

“I’ve hit him harder while educating him,” Severan said. “He just tries to argue.”

Sabine’s mouth shifted by perhaps a millimeter. That appeared to be her current allowance for humor.

Daniel looked down.

Thick paper covered the front of the bench. Inked lines showed a vertical shaft, a pump housing, and assemblies Daniel could not identify. A black ink pot sat near one corner, with brass filings scattered farther back. Behind the drawing, a slotted brass frame lay partly assembled among the loose components. One opening matched the fitting Daniel had knocked aside.

At the back-right of the bench, a cloudy red sphere rested within a broad cradle of brass and blackened steel. Heavy clamps held it in place. Pale blue-white light traced channels over its surface and into the surrounding brasswork. Beside the cradle, the shaft and gearing remained still.

No. I know what that is.

I am looking at a harvested mana core.

I do not know how any of this works. I only know that errors could kill someone.

The drawing showed a canal pump powered by the mana core. Etched channels directed mana through a regulator into the surrounding mechanism, while the shaft and gearing transmitted the resulting motion to the pump. That part of the design made sense; Daniel had made his living as a mechanical engineer. How mana started the system moving remained beyond him.

A muted wonder stirred beneath the fear. He was looking at the kind of impossible mechanism that had belonged to fantasy on Earth.

Severan tapped the ruler against the unfinished drawing. “Do you remember where you left off?”

Daniel’s natural response was to admit he had no idea. He searched for something Lucan might say instead, but found nothing.

“No.”

Sabine set down her file with deliberate care.

Severan’s gaze moved to the smear of ink where Daniel’s cheek had rested. “You fell asleep at an active bench. Not knowing where you stopped makes it worse.”

Nerio shifted the coil beneath one arm. “He’s awake now. Give him a moment to find his feet.”

I can explain the pain. Not the rest.

“Stand,” Severan said.

Daniel pushed himself to his feet. His center of gravity was lower than expected, his shoulders broader, his legs more responsive. He caught the bench, but Severan saw the sway.

Severan set the ruler on the bench, gripped Daniel’s chin, and turned his face into the nearest sunbeam. Dust drifted between them while the master checked his eyes and scalp without gentleness.

“Eyes clear. No bleeding. Can you count?”

“Yes.”

“An improvement already.”

Nerio coughed into his fist, stifling a laugh.

Severan released him. “Then count the failures in the regulator you drew before we get to discover them underwater.”

Severan slid the diagram toward Daniel.

Daniel studied it again. Unmarked revisions crossed one another, and precise dimensions gave way abruptly to estimates. The drive had no way to disengage if the regulator seized. A valve lacked a visible relief path if it jammed. The core cradle lay close enough to the transmission shaft that a failed bearing could take both with it.

The mechanical risks were clear. The etched channels remained an unknown.

“At least three potential mechanical failures,” Daniel said. “More if those channels respond to heat.”

Silence.

Severan’s brows lowered. “They do.”

“Then there are more,” Daniel said.

“How many?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Sabine’s stare sharpened.

I won’t guess just to sound like whoever this Lucan is.

Severan studied Daniel. “You usually answer before I finish asking.”

“I can guess now,” Daniel said, “or check the channel layout and whatever test records you keep for the core.”

“The core rating,” Severan corrected. “Sit. Stay awake. Check it against the channel layout. Until then, you do not touch the core or its channels.”

Daniel sat.

For the next several hours, Daniel settled into the deliberate rhythm of his old work and refused to move faster than his understanding.

He separated what he knew from what he did not, then broke each unknown into a question. The drawings and half-built mechanisms gave him one answer: removable regulating plates divided or restricted the mana carried through the carved channels. He did not yet understand the rules, but he could see the danger. A bad carving could stop one part, overload another, or send mana where it could do real damage.

Once the work had settled into manageable questions, Daniel allowed himself to study the workshop around him. Tools lined the walls beneath smoke-darkened beams, and mechanisms in various stages of repair filled the open bays. The canal showed through tall shuttered doors. Deeper inside, the benches stood farther apart, and heavy doors divided the work areas. Bare floor isolated the shielded test stands from the rest of the workshop.

He recognized a stripped floodgate mechanism and a sediment-scored pump almost at once. Warming plates, cooling chests, lamps, and weapon fittings crowded the smaller benches. But a nest of brass rings around an empty core socket told him nothing, nor did a sealed ceramic cylinder with no visible controls. Artificing plainly extended far beyond the civic project before him.

A hammer rang above the steady rasp of files. From below, water knocked against the foundations. Sabine drew a narrow file through the grooves of a regulating plate, pausing after each pass to check their depth. At the next bench, Nerio turned the shaft of a small gear assembly by hand and listened to the teeth engage. Severan paused at their benches only long enough to correct them, then passed Daniel in silence.

Daniel kept his attention on the regulator plan.

Sabine kept her attention on the plate. “You drew it yesterday. Find the bypass, trace it from the pressure line back to the intake, and do not mark anything you have not verified.”

Daniel followed the pressure line across the drawing. A thinner branch left it just before the valve and looped back toward the intake—the bypass. He checked every connection back to the intake, then marked the bypass. “Thank you.”

Sabine finally looked at him. “You are thanking me for stopping you from guessing?”

“For correcting me. I would rather minimize the number of mistakes I make.”

The corner of Sabine’s mouth twitched. “Reasonable.”

Later, Nerio set three regulating plates within reach, safely clear of the red core. “Take your time. This design needs the flow divided. Which one?”

Daniel studied the plates but did not touch them. Grooves radiated across the first like spokes. The grooves on the second split into smaller branches, while the last tightened into a spiral his eyes resisted following.

The system was new; the problem was not. Power had to leave its source and reach the parts that used it without damaging anything along the way. On Earth, wires, pipes, and valves had carried and controlled that flow. As Nerio turned the branching plate, Daniel traced its grooves from one path into several. Whatever mana was, it still had to be guided.

“The branching one,” Daniel said. “It divides the flow.”

“And for this core?”

Daniel looked at the cloudy red surface in its cradle. “I don’t know.”

Surprise crossed Nerio’s face. He turned the plate over and tapped two tiny notches beside the rim. “Start with the core rating. Those marks tell you what the plate was cut to tolerate.”

Apparently, Lucan would rather guess than admit he did not know.

“I’ll check,” Daniel added.

As the last light turned the high windows amber, Severan dismissed Sabine and Nerio and stopped Daniel at the door. He pressed the stained regulator drawing into his hands.

“Redo it.”

Severan handed him two more sheets. “Identify every failure path you can justify. Order the tests so they risk neither the core, the mechanism, nor anyone nearby. Have it on my bench before work begins tomorrow.”

Daniel took the sheets without complaint. “It will be ready.”

Severan lingered for a moment, as though expecting more, then moved on.

That, at least, is a language I understand.

Outside, Nerio caught up with him along the workshop’s stone arcade.

“Before you go,” Nerio said, “I need to know whether you can get home safely.”

Sabine strode past them. “He can walk. What he should not do is touch another tool until that assignment is finished.”

Daniel said nothing. Nerio paused and studied him, waiting for a retort that did not come.

“You all right?”

Not in any way I can explain.

“My head hurts.”

That much was true.

Nerio nodded once. “Go home, then. I’ll finish closing the test bay. Keep to the streets, and sit down if the dizziness worsens—preferably before you reach a canal.”

The way home should have been impossible. Yet each turn felt familiar when he reached it, as though his feet had made the route hundreds of times before his mind caught up.

A narrow street led away from Corvane’s workshop and the lock complex, squeezed between vaulted warehouses whose open bays smelled of damp rope, sawn timber, grain dust, and river mud. Beyond, a market arcade hung with striped awnings around a fountain court where three lanes met. Nothing showed him what waited past it. The sloping streets made the body’s balance harder to manage, forcing him to shorten his steps near the canal edges.

Varenza—the city Lucan had called home. The name came easily; the rest Daniel could see.

From the bridge, Daniel could see the Velis River descending from the mountains into Varenza. Within the city, locks divided its flow among narrow canals. Barges pressed toward mills and warehouses while smaller channels slipped between red-tiled homes and courtyards. Corvane’s workshop lay behind him near one of the principal locks. At the next corner, recognition drew him toward older, higher ground.

They had made the waterworks part of the city’s beauty rather than hiding them.

Clear green water pressed against the lock gates, escaping in thin streams through their seams. Mineral lines marked old floods above the present flow; crews cleared silt from an overflow basin. A core-lit lifting frame hauled a dripping basket onto the quay while ropes strained and two workers steadied the load by hand.

Mana is integrated into the machinery. I wonder how many other applications are possible.

Beyond the bridge, pale limestone facades rose from brick foundations to red-tiled roofs. Arcades shaded shopfronts and mosaic thresholds. On the lowest plots, doorways began above broad stone platforms, and hairline cracks marked where the waterlogged ground had settled. The soundest buildings stood true. The city had been built by people who understood how quickly water punished neglect.

Merchants bargained in the shade of the colonnades while neighbors traded news across the water from balconies crowded with cloth and herb pots. Twice people greeted Lucan by name. A canal worker saluted; an older fruit seller asked after Tessa without waiting for more than Daniel’s cautious nod. In Varenza, family reputation traveled faster than any boat.

Farther on, a public fountain spilled into a basin tiled blue and green. Children trailed their fingers through the overflow while merchants packed the stalls.

Sunlight flashed from the water.

The glare stirred a memory of a grand hotel ballroom, crowded with well-dressed guests beneath crystal chandeliers.

It came with the dry chill of conditioned air and the ache of an evening he had attended only because declining would have required more explanation than appearing.

At thirty-two, he had come straight from work to the reception, wearing the dark jacket he kept in his office for formal events. Amber lights glowed above polished floors. Music and conversation folded into a soft, expensive roar. Servers moved between groups with bright glasses on black trays, and the city beyond the windows glittered without revealing a single star.

He had checked his phone twice in five minutes when Claire Bennett joined him. She had the practiced poise of someone accustomed to the spotlight.

Her black hair and dark brows stood in sharp contrast to her fair skin. Her expression was composed, more reserved than stern. The clean lines of her dress revealed her toned shoulders and arms.

Her athletic career had shaped that strength and the precise control with which she carried herself.

Quiet amusement softened her eyes and smile, and the polished public version of her became the Claire he had come to know over the years. Their conversations had a habit of resuming where the last one had ended, as though the time between them had been only a small interruption.

“There’s my lifesaver.”

“Glad to see you’re doing well.”

The nickname came from their previous meeting, more than a year earlier. After a charity gala, Daniel had come across Claire standing beside her stalled car in the hotel garage. He had traced the fault under the hood and gotten the engine running again.

Before driving away, she had thanked him with the same lightly amused warmth she wore now.

Back in the ballroom, they stood near the room’s edge while laughter threaded through the music and glasses chimed on passing trays.

Then the amber lights gave way to something brighter.

White flooded the windows, the polished floor, the moving crowd. Voices broke into startled cries. Daniel’s eyes moved toward Claire through the glare and met hers.

The crowd blurred into pale silhouettes and vanished. Claire remained for one suspended heartbeat, her face the only shape left in the world, before the white swallowed her too.

Then pain. A wooden ruler. A stranger’s blonde hair falling across his eyes.

End of Chapter 1
